## At a glance

Hiroshi Sugimoto is a Japanese artist whose practice began with large-format, predominantly black-and-white photography and expanded into sculpture, architecture, installation, collecting, writing, and the direction of traditional performing arts. Since the 1970s, he has used serial structures and technically exacting image-making to examine time, perception, historical representation, and the uncertain boundary between an original and its reproduction. His best-known ongoing bodies of work include *Dioramas* (from 1976), *Theaters* (from 1978), and *Seascapes* (from 1980). [S3][S5][S7]

His institutional position is extensively documented. Major surveys include the Hirshhorn/Mori Art Museum retrospective initiated in 2005–06 and *Time Machine*, presented at Hayward Gallery in 2023 before touring to UCCA Beijing and the Museum of Contemporary Art Australia in 2024. As of the report cutoff, *Extinction* is on view at the National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo, and *Form Is Emptiness* at Singapore Art Museum. [S2][S6][S11][S12]

## Biography and career development

Sugimoto was born in Tokyo in 1948. His official biography records graduation from Saint Paul’s University in Tokyo in 1970 and from Art Center College of Design in Los Angeles in 1974, followed by a move to New York that year. Current institutional and gallery sources describe him as living and working between Tokyo and New York. [S1][S5][S17]

Photography initially provided the core structure of his practice. In New York, museum displays, cinemas, the ocean horizon, wax figures, and modernist buildings became laboratories through which he tested what a photograph records: an event in the world, a constructed illusion, elapsed time, or a culturally inherited image. His use of an 8 × 10-inch large-format camera, long exposures, controlled focus, and carefully developed gelatin-silver printing belongs to the material history of photography while serving conceptual questions. [S3][S5][S7]

From the 1990s and especially the 2000s, the practice broadened. *Portraits* revisited historical figures through photographs of wax reconstructions; *Architecture* deliberately blurred canonical buildings; *Conceptual Forms* translated mathematical models into photographs and sculpture; *Lightning Fields* applied electrical discharge directly to film; and *Photogenic Drawings* reworked early negatives by William Henry Fox Talbot. [S5][S7][S16]

This expansion became organizational and architectural as well as artistic. Sugimoto founded the New Material Research Laboratory in 2008 and the Odawara Art Foundation in 2009. The Foundation’s Enoura Observatory, conceived by Sugimoto and opened to the public in 2017, combines gallery, Noh stage, tea room, garden, historic architectural fragments, and astronomical alignments. It functions not simply as a building containing his work but as a long-term synthesis of art, architecture, collecting, craft, landscape, and performance. [S13]

Selected honors include the Mainichi Art Prize (1988), Hasselblad Foundation International Award in Photography (2001), Praemium Imperiale (2009), Japan’s Medal with Purple Ribbon (2010), Officier de l’Ordre des Arts et des Lettres (2013), the Royal Photographic Society Centenary Medal (2017), and recognition as a Person of Cultural Merit in Japan (2017). [S1][S13]

## Artistic practice

### Documented methods and concerns

- **Time as photographic material:** In the *Theaters*, a complete film is recorded in a single long exposure. The screen becomes white while the duration of the film illuminates the architecture around it. [S3]
- **Serial comparison:** *Seascapes* holds the horizon near the center while location, weather, atmosphere, and exposure change. The repeated structure makes difference visible without conventional narrative. [S5][S9]
- **Constructed reality:** *Dioramas* isolates museum displays from labels and surrounding architecture so that artificial habitats can appear plausibly alive. *Portraits* photograph wax reconstructions using conventions inherited from painted portraiture. [S7]
- **Optical uncertainty:** In the *Architecture* series, intentional defocus reduces canonical buildings to remembered or conceptual forms rather than descriptive records. [S10]
- **Image without a camera:** *Lightning Fields* uses high-voltage electrical discharge directly on film, while *Photogenic Drawings* enlarges and reinterprets early photographic negatives. Both extend photography beyond conventional camera exposure. [S4][S7]
- **Material continuity:** Sugimoto repeatedly emphasizes traditional photographic craft, especially gelatin-silver printing and tonal control, even while applying it to conceptual problems. [S5][S11]
- **Expansion beyond photography:** Architecture, sculpture, performance direction, antiquities, Japanese craft, religious and literary traditions, and astronomical orientation have become increasingly integrated into the practice. [S12][S13][S14]

### Artist statements

Sugimoto has described photography as a form of time machine and has repeatedly framed his series through questions: whether a whole film can be photographed in one frame; whether a contemporary viewer can see a sea comparable to that seen by early humans; and whether the camera records reality or a constructed belief. These statements are not explanations added after the fact—they often establish the experimental rule from which a series is produced. [S3][S5][S20]

### Institutional interpretations

The Getty reads *Dioramas*, *Portraits*, and *Photogenic Drawings* as investigations into how visual representation constructs and distills history. The Hirshhorn describes the major photographic series as inquiries into time, space, culture, perception, and the nature of reality. Singapore Art Museum positions the current survey around the Heart Sutra proposition that form and emptiness are interdependent, connecting photographic appearance to the definitions through which reality is perceived. [S6][S7][S12]

### System synthesis

Across media, Sugimoto does not treat photography as a neutral witness. He builds controlled situations in which an image becomes evidence of something that cannot be seen directly: duration, memory, belief, historical distance, electrical force, or infinity. The method remains consistent even as the media expand: define a conceptual proposition, reduce the visual variables, execute it with exceptional material control, and let small differences destabilize what initially appears self-evident. This is a system synthesis supported by the artist’s project descriptions and museum interpretations, not a quotation or a single institution’s verdict. [S3][S5–S7][S12–S14]

## Practice timeline

The phases below describe expansion and overlap, not the abandonment of earlier series.

### 1. Constructed reality and accumulated time, 1976–1979

*Dioramas* (from 1976) and *Theaters* (from 1978) established two enduring procedures: photographing staged representations as if they were real and using long exposure to compress duration. Representative works include *Polar Bear* (1976) and *U.A. Playhouse, New York* (1978). [S3][S7][S16]

### 2. Horizon, repetition, and deep time, 1980–mid-1990s

Beginning in 1980, *Seascapes* reduced the image to sea, sky, horizon, light, and location. The series asks whether a modern viewer can encounter a scene that materially resembles one available to early humans. During the same period, Sugimoto continued *Dioramas* and *Theaters*, making serial comparison rather than stylistic replacement central to the practice. [S5][S9][S20]

### 3. Reproduced history and unstable icons, mid-1990s–early 2000s

*Portraits* and *Architecture* shifted attention toward culturally inherited images. Wax figures were photographed as historical sitters, while modernist buildings were intentionally defocused until they resembled memories or mental templates. *Sea of Buddha* also brought serial photography into dialogue with Buddhist sculpture and repetition. [S7][S10][S16]

### 4. Science, pre-photographic history, and direct inscription, 2004–2010s

*Conceptual Forms*, *Lightning Fields*, and *Photogenic Drawings* connected photography to mathematical models, electricity, and the origins of the medium. Photography became alternately a record of abstract form, a surface physically struck by electrical force, and a means of translating nineteenth-century negatives. [S4][S7][S16]

### 5. Integrated cultural practice, 2008–present

With the New Material Research Laboratory, Odawara Art Foundation, Enoura Observatory, public sculpture, performance direction, and projects based on Japanese literary and religious traditions, Sugimoto increasingly created settings and institutions rather than isolated images alone. Photography remains central—as the 2026 *Extinction* exhibition emphasizes—but now operates within a larger ecology of architecture, objects, landscape, ritual, and performance. [S11–S14]
